A watch face that cannot be built

The day has two halves. Your watch knows one.

wijzer is a 24-hour dial that draws your calendar and your sky together — meetings as arcs, the sun, moon and tide resting at their true hours — so you can see how much of the day is still yours. It works. It cannot ship, because watchOS does not open the face.

I. The dial

One revolution is one day.

Midnight at the top, noon at the bottom, the hand going round once. Your meetings sit inside as arcs. Under the rim, as if the watch had been laid down on its own almanac, rest the sun, the moon and the tide — each on the hour it actually happens. You stop reading a list and start reading a shape.

Dear Apple,

You have built the best calendar I wear. The next event, the minutes until it, the tap on the wrist that arrives exactly when it should. I am not writing to complain about any of it.

I am writing because a calendar is a ledger of what you owe other people. It knows the meeting and not the morning — not when the sun comes up, when the tide turns, or whether the moon will be out when you finally close the laptop. A watch built only on the calendar shows a full day and an empty one and makes them look identical. And every wellbeing feature grades me afterwards; none of them help with the question asked in the morning: how much of today is mine, and when?

Balance is not a number you close at midnight. It is seeing the whole shape of a day early enough to do something about it.

The finding

Dinner ends around seven. Forty-three minutes after sunset, a moon one day past full rises over a spring tide — the widest range of the month. Neither half of the day contains that fact. Only the dial does, on the same revolution as the standup.

II. Try the faces

One watch. Three faces. A swipe apart.

On the wrist the faces are one surface: the day, the shore, the schedule. This is the working prototype at true proportions — swipe the screen, turn the crown, or pick a face.

  1. i.The Dial Everything at once: arcs for the meetings, glyphs for the sky, the tide at the rim, and one black hand carrying the time. 8:55 Thursday. Standup in five minutes; the tide turns at 10:22, and it will be raining when it does.
  2. ii.Outdoor The meetings fall away. The sun crosses its arc above a true tide curve, cosine-interpolated between the day’s four extremes. 3.7 feet and rising. High at 10:22, then the long fall to 4:37. The moon waits for its 8:50 rise.
  3. iii.Work The sky falls away. Five slots, a red rule for now, and how long you have — on a Swiss grid where the hour axis and the type share columns. The mode you leave, not the one you live in. 3.75 hours booked of 24; free after seven.

III. A ledger of revisions

What your guidelines changed.

I took the watchOS guidance to the dial rather than to the argument. Eleven measurable changes, all in the faces above — because a letter asking you to open the face ought to prove it can be trusted with one.

The glyph row was my mistake, not yours. An earlier draft showed a 67% moon rising fifteen minutes before sunset — not a thing the sky does — and nudged two glyphs 2.5° off their hours. Computing the real ephemeris fixed both: the moon became a day past full, and every glyph went back onto its exact hour.

And the type is still not large enough. At 10.5 px on the 364-point study, fitted to an Ultra’s 205 points, the graduation lands near 5.9 pt — half of Caption2. The shipping dial drops the temperature graduation and promotes the sun and moon glyphs into the space.

IV. The ask

Why none of this can ship — and three ways it could.

There is no third-party watch face API, and there never has been. WidgetKit complications render accented — effectively two-tone — which is fatal to a design whose argument is that calendar color, water blue and a brass daylight arc mean different things. A shared .watchface file configures one of your faces; it carries no custom art. An app behind Return to Clock is not a face, and should not pretend to be.

Yet the same design is buildable on Wear OS this afternoon, because Watch Face Format exists: declarative, sandboxed, no arbitrary code at render time, and cheap enough on power that Google made it the only supported route. The battery-and-jank worry has been answered — by a constrained format, not a closed door.

i.
Costs you least

Full-color accessory rendering

Let a complication opt into its own palette the way a widget can. Even a narrow, reviewed allowance with a monochrome fallback you require would let much of this dial exist inside your faces tomorrow.

ii.
Leaves the face alone

A larger canvas in the Smart Stack

The rectangular family is about a quarter of the screen, and a 24-hour dial needs a circle. A full-screen slot reached by the crown — never replacing your clock — would carry almost all of this.

iii.
The real ask

A declarative face format

Not a code-executing face — a described one. Arcs, glyphs, hands, complication slots, a fixed refresh budget, review like any app. You keep the power envelope, the frame budget and the taste bar; the rest of us bring you dials you have not thought of.

V. In closing

This watch does not exist. Yet.

A design study — prototyped in HTML and SVG at true widget scale, refined through six aesthetic directions and five permutations to the dial above, and revised once more against your own guidance. The sky is computed. The tides are a real shape. The only fictional part is that anyone can wear it.
