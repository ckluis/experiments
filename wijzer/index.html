A WATCH FACE THAT CANNOT BE BUILT

The day has two halves.
Your watch knows one.

A calendar records what you owe other people. It has nothing to say about when the light goes, when the tide turns, or how much of the day is still yours. wijzer draws both halves on one dial — and then runs into the fact that watchOS does not open the face.

THE LETTER

Dear Apple,

You have built the best calendar I wear. The next event, the minutes until it, the tap on the wrist that arrives exactly when it should — nothing else on my desk or in my pocket does that as well. I am not writing to complain about any of it.

I am writing because a calendar can only ever be a ledger of what you owe other people. It knows the meeting and not the morning. It has no opinion about when the sun comes up, when the tide turns, or whether the moon will be out when you finally close the laptop. It records obligation and is silent on everything else — which means a watch built only on the calendar will show you a full day and an empty one and make them look identical.

Every wellbeing feature I have ever used works the same way: it counts what I did and grades me afterwards. Rings, hours, minutes of movement. Useful, and always retrospective. None of them help with the actual question, which is asked in the morning and sounds like how much of today is mine, and when.

Balance is not a number you close at midnight. It is being able to see the whole shape of a day early enough to do something about it.

wijzer puts both halves on one dial. Twenty-four hours, one revolution, midnight at the top. Your meetings sit as colored arcs at their true hours. Beneath the rim, at the hours they occur, rest the sun, the moon and the tide — as if the watch had been laid down on its own almanac.

The point was never more data on a small screen. The point is proportion. When the whole day is in one revolution, you stop reading a list and start reading a shape: this much is spoken for, this much is not, and the unspoken part has weather and light and a tide in it.

The Thursday on this page

Rather than argue the case, let me show you an ordinary working day. What follows is a real one — Thursday, 30 July 2026, at 41.5° north on the New England coast. The sky is computed, not invented: sunrise, sunset, moonrise, moonset and phase all come out of the ephemeris for that date and that place. The calendar half is the sort of Thursday most people reading this have had.

The finding

Dinner ends around seven. Forty-three minutes after sunset, a moon one day past full comes up over a spring tide — and because that moon is full, the sea is running its widest range of the month. Neither half of the day contains that fact. Only the dial does. The evening is not free time in the abstract; it is a specific, findable, ninety-minute thing, and on wijzer it sits on the same revolution as the standup.

THE COMPLICATIONS

Four complications. One dial.

In watchmaking, a complication is anything a watch does beyond telling the time. wijzer carries four. Only the first of them exists on a wrist today.

No. I

THE DAY

Twenty-four hours on one revolution, midnight at the top. Events sit as colored arcs at their true hours — the shape of the day, not just the next thing in it, and therefore the gaps as clearly as the commitments.

No. II

SUN & MOON

Sunrise, sunset, moonrise and moonset rest beneath the dial's edge at the hours they occur, as if the watch were laid upon its own almanac. The moon is drawn phase-correct to the day — here, one day past full.

No. III

THE TIDE

Two highs and two lows, felt at the rim in water blue. When rain meets the morning high, the two glyphs share a spoke and read as one moment. A full moon widens the range; the dial shows that without saying it.

No. IV

TEMPERATURE

A quiet graduation rings the dial every six hours, at the cardinals, on one radius. Four readings where there were six, and the sun's own degree labels struck out entirely: the ledger of revisions below shows what that cost.

Plate I

The Dial.

Everything at once. The full instrument for a day that holds both a standup and a spring tide — arcs for the meetings, glyphs for the sky, the tide at the rim, and a single black hand carrying the time alone.

PLATE III

Work.

The sky falls away. Five slots in their calendar colors, a red rule for now, and how long you have — on a strict Swiss grid where the hour axis and the typography share the same columns.

This is not the mode you live in. It is the mode you leave, and it is built to answer one question quickly so you can stop looking at it.

Standup in five minutes. Three and three-quarter hours booked out of twenty-four. Free after seven.

Two of those deserve saying plainly. The glyph row is not your doing — it is mine. The earlier draft showed a 67% moon rising fifteen minutes before sunset, which is not a thing the sky does; only a full moon rises at sunset. Two glyphs had been nudged 2.5° off their true hours to stop them fusing. Computing the real ephemeris fixed both at once: the moon became a day past full, the crowding dissolved, and every glyph went back onto its exact hour. A design that tells the truth about the sky turned out to need fewer compromises, not more.

And the type is still not large enough. At 10.5 px on a 364-point study, fitted to an Ultra's 205 points, the graduation lands near 5.9 pt — half of Caption2. I am not going to pretend otherwise. Closing that gap is not a scaling problem, it is an editing problem: the shipping dial drops the temperature graduation entirely and promotes the four sun and moon glyphs into the space. I would rather show you the study honestly and tell you what it costs than show you a render that has never met a real display.

Which brings me to the ask

None of this can ship, and I want to be precise about why rather than vague about it. There is no third-party watch face API, and there never has been. Complications through WidgetKit are real and I use them, but the accessory families render accented — effectively two-tone — which is fatal to a design whose whole argument is that calendar color and water blue and a brass daylight arc mean different things. A shared .watchface file carries a configuration of one of your faces, not custom art. The only honest path open to me today is an app behind Return to Clock, which is not a face and should not pretend to be.

Meanwhile the same design is buildable on Wear OS this afternoon, because Watch Face Format exists: declarative, sandboxed, no arbitrary code at render time, reviewable, and cheap enough on power that Google made it the only supported route. That is the existence proof. The concern that third-party faces mean battery disasters and janky hands has already been answered by a constrained format rather than a closed door.

So, three requests, smallest first.

i.

FULL-COLOR ACCESSORY RENDERING

Let a complication opt into its own palette the way a widget can. Even a narrow allowance — an opt-in, reviewed, with a monochrome fallback you require — would let a great deal of this dial exist inside your faces tomorrow. This is the change that costs you least.

ii.

A LARGER CANVAS IN THE SMART STACK

The rectangular family is about a quarter of the screen. A 24-hour dial needs a circle. If the face itself stays closed, a full-screen slot in the Smart Stack — reached by the crown, never replacing your clock — would carry almost all of this without touching the face at all.

iii.

A DECLARATIVE FACE FORMAT

The real ask. Not a code-executing face — a described one. Arcs, glyphs, hands, complication slots, a fixed refresh budget, review like any app. You would keep control of the power envelope, the frame budget and the taste bar, and the rest of us would get to bring you dials you have not thought of. This is the one I actually want.

IN CLOSING

This watch does not exist. Yet.

wijzer is a design study — prototyped entirely in HTML at true widget scale, refined through six aesthetic directions and five permutations to the dial above, and now revised once more against your own guidance. The sky in it is computed. The tides are a real shape. The only fictional part is that anyone can wear it.

I would build this properly the day there is a way to. Until then it is a letter, and a working prototype, and an argument that the wrist is the right place to see a whole day at once — including the part of it that nobody has booked.
