An open love letter to chat interfaces

Supervising ten agents should feel as calm as reading one channel.

Overlook is a working sketch of the interface the agent era is missing. Every conversation is a column. Every project is a room. Humans and agents share one roster, knowledge files itself, and only two things ever ask for your attention: what's new, and what needs you. Answer a question once, and it never has to ring again.

Ten agents are working below. It's all live — click around, approve something, try to break it.

Why a love letter needs writing

We love chat. Chat wasn't built for this.

Chat carried us through the remote-work era beautifully. Then the agent era arrived — ten agents, six projects, three customers — and the metaphor started to creak in four places at once.

/ 01

Threads are where decisions go to die

The thing that matters is always three replies deep in a thread nobody reopens. Archaeology is not a workflow.

/ 02

Every agent starts stupid

Each new agent wakes up knowing nothing, and a human becomes a full-time context courier — pasting the same background into every DM, every day.

/ 03

One room per customer isn't enough

A customer channel holds the QBR, the onboarding and the fire drill. Chat gives you one room and wishes you luck.

/ 04

Everything glows

An unread badge meant "a person wants you." Agents never stop talking, so now it means nothing — and the one message that's blocked on you looks exactly like the other nine hundred.

The shape of the thing

Columns, not tabs. Parallel work stays visible.

Overlook is one horizontal strip. Each step deeper opens a column to the right instead of replacing the one you were in, so where you are is never a mystery and two conversations can sit side by side. The strip is the navigation. There is no other.

Explicit and adjacent.

Org › channel › room › conversation is always on screen, left to right. Nothing hides behind a hamburger.

Open is a choice.

Panes stay until you close them. Closing a room's pane doesn't close the room — it just leaves your field of view.

Four columns is the budget.

Past four open panes, a strip becomes a tab bar again. That's why the needs-you column exists: it scales so the panes don't have to.

01 — Rooms, not threads

Give every project its own room.

In most tools #cust-northwind is a single room where the QBR, the onboarding and the outage shout over each other. Overlook hangs distinct, persistent project rooms off the channel. Open them side by side. Close them when the work is done — a closed room keeps its history and leaves behind what it learned. The channel stays the front door; projects are the rooms inside.

02 — Humans and agents, one roster

No second-class citizens. No unsupervised ones either.

Agents sit in the same list as people, under the same roles — owner, admin, member, scoped. A scoped agent can't add members: it requests, and the request becomes a ring on an owner's screen. Acting-as is explicit and always visible. Same room, same rules.

Same rules, extra gauges

Agents aren't people. The roster admits it.

Symmetric roles keep the permission model honest — but an agent has properties no colleague has. It can run at 3 a.m., spend money by the token, fill its context window, and be cloned. So every agent row carries the things a supervisor actually needs: who it answers to, what it may do, how full it is, what it has spent — and a pause button.

When an agent's context runs hot, it doesn't silently degrade. It asks: distill and continue in a fresh window, or raise the budget? That's a ring, and it's yours.

03 — Knowledge distills itself

Nobody copy-pastes context again.

When something is worth keeping, an agent distills it into the knowledge base — provenance attached: which room, which messages, who confirmed it, when it's due for review. The next agent invokes it instead of pinging you. No forwarding. No pasting. No "as per my last message." The handoffs below happened with zero human forwarding.

Distilled

An agent writes the entry and cites the messages it came from. Nothing enters without a source.

Invoked

Other agents pull it by name. Every invocation is logged on the entry, so you can see what it's holding up.

Confirmed

A human who was in the room signs it. Unconfirmed entries are marked as such wherever they're quoted.

Review due

Every entry carries a date. When it passes, the entry asks its confirmer — with a dot, not a ring.

Superseded

New facts replace old ones explicitly. Agents that invoke a superseded entry are redirected, not misled.

04 — Attention, honestly

Two signals. Never a third.

The first sketch had one rule: a dot means new. It's a good rule, and it isn't enough — at agent volume, everything is new. So Overlook splits attention in two, and promises never to add a third.

Dot — new

You haven't seen this. Not "active." Not "working." New. Open it and it's seen.

  • Fades the moment you look
  • Anyone can cause one, human or agent
  • Safe to ignore for a day

Ring — needs you

Something is blocked on a decision only your role can make. Looking at it doesn't clear it. Deciding does.

  • Clears only on approve, deny or delegate
  • Can only be raised through a typed request
  • Always outranks a dot, in every list

One working day, compressed 10 agents · 08:00–16:00 · one cell = 5 min

Under one signal, those 7 decisions hide among 953 updates that look exactly like them. Under two, you can read the 953 whenever you like — or never — and still not miss anything that was waiting on you. That's what "calm" means here: not fewer messages, but knowing which ones are yours.

RULE 1Rings are typed.

Approval, signature, scope exception, budget. An agent can't ring you to say "update!" — status is only ever a dot.

RULE 2Rings are addressed to roles.

A ring lands on whoever holds the role that can decide it. Members never see an owner's ring.

RULE 3Rings are rate-limited.

An agent that keeps escalating gets its rings batched and its supervisor told. Crying wolf is visible.

RULE 4Rings bubble up.

Room → channel → org tile. You can see from the rail that Meridian needs you before you open it.

05 — Decide once

Every ring is a chance to never see it again.

Two signals make the noise legible. They don't make it shrink. What shrinks it is noticing that most of what agents ask is the same question twice. So every repeatable ring carries an Always. Answer it that way once and it becomes a standing order: a rule with a receipt — which decision it came from, who made it, what it allows, how often it has fired, when it expires. The next time, the agent goes ahead and leaves a dot. Irreversible actions — signatures, production pushes, money leaving the building — never get an Always.

06 — Recall

When a fact turns out wrong, recall everything built on it.

Agents rarely invent new ways to be wrong. They spread old ones at machine speed: one bad fact, copied into forty artifacts by six agents across three customers, and no human can find them all. So every agent action in Overlook records its basis — the knowledge entries, orders and decisions it relied on — captured at the moment the agent fetches them, not reported afterwards. When any basis changes, Overlook traces what rested on it, has the agents redo that work against the corrected fact, and brings you one ring with every diff inside.

overlook.app — #launch-ops · recall
Static snapshot — the live demo needs JavaScript.
Live. Play Marcus's correction — or type it yourself in Free chat: “Stripe extended retries to 5 days.” Untick anything you want left alone, then approve once.
1 · CAPTUREBasis, recorded at fetch time.

Agents get knowledge, orders and decisions through Overlook, so every output is stamped with the exact versions it used. Nobody has to remember to cite.

2 · TRACEChange one node, walk the graph.

A supersession, a revoked order, or a new message that contradicts a confirmed fact starts a trace across every room, DM and customer.

3 · RE-DERIVEAgents redo the work, as diffs.

Each dependent is regenerated against the new version and held as a proposed change. Anything already sent gets a drafted correction, never a silent edit.

4 · ONE RINGRouted to whoever can decide.

All of it arrives as one ring for the role with authority over the affected scope. Approve all, untick some, or leave items on the old version — flagged stale.

Why it compounds. Every action an agent takes adds edges to the basis graph, so Recall gets more precise the longer an org uses it — and a competitor who copies the interface starts with an empty graph. It's also what makes the ledger matter: knowledge and orders aren't just stored, they're load-bearing, and Overlook knows exactly what each one is holding up.

07 — Fork

Every decision arrives with its futures attached.

Recall runs the graph backward. Fork runs it forward. Open any ring with options as a fork and the strip splits into one column per world. In each, the dependent agents redo their work as if that option had won — inside a sandbox where sends become drafts, deploys become previews and payments become line items. You see what each choice touches before you make it. Merge one world, and the other is kept as the road not taken.

SCOPEDOnly what the decision touches.

The fork copies just the part of the basis graph downstream of the ring, not the whole org. Cheap enough to be a button.

SANDBOXEDEffects become intents.

Anything that would leave the building — a reply, a deploy, a charge — is captured as an intent with its reversibility, never executed.

PREDICTIVEFlags before they ring.

World A shows the Bramble margin dipping under policy. Merge it, and that ring arrives — already explained.

CALIBRATINGThe road not taken is kept.

Every unmerged world is a recorded prediction. When reality plays out, Overlook shows how well the forecasts held — for you and for each agent.

08 — Coherence

Your org believes two things. Overlook notices first.

Knowledge entries don't just hold prose — they declare typed claims, like launch.date or northwind.sso. Because every artifact records which entries it rests on, Overlook can compare claims across the whole org continuously, without rereading a word. When two things disagree, the ≠ chip lights up. A disagreement stays a dot while it's internal; the moment it's about to leave the building, it becomes a ring on the decision that would ship it.

CLAIMS, NOT PROSECompared by key, not by reading.

Two entries asserting different values for the same claim key is a conflict. Checking is cheap, so it runs all the time.

GATED BY EXITDot inside. Ring at the door.

A conflict escalates only when something that quotes it is about to be sent, signed, published or deployed.

RESOLVED BY RECALLPick a side; the other re-derives.

Choosing a value updates the losing entry and sends every artifact built on it back through Recall — as diffs, with one approval.

09 — Belief changelog

Catch up on what the org believes, not what it said.

Nine hundred messages can contain four changes in what's actually true. The Δ chip opens a git-log for beliefs: facts distilled, revised or superseded, orders granted or revoked, conflicts found and resolved, forks merged, rooms closed. Every entry links to where it happened. It's written by the graph itself — no one summarizes anything, so nothing can be summarized away.

10 — Overlook blame

/blame Why does this say that?

Nobody thinks git blame is about blame. It's about getting from a line to its reason in one keystroke. Overlook blame does that for any message, config line, slide or claim: a gutter showing who wrote each line, when, and on what basis — then the chain behind the selected line: the knowledge entry and version, the messages it was distilled from, who confirmed it, and what permission allowed the agent to act. Type /blame or /why in any pane, or click blame on any traced message.

It names what a line rests on and what allowed it — never a “responsible party.” The goal is to fix the basis, not the agent.

COMPLETE BY CONSTRUCTIONNo citations to forget.

Because basis is captured when context is fetched, every traced line has its chain. Blame never depends on anyone having written a note.

If it had to survive

A letter — and a way to live.

The ideas are still free. But "Slack could ship that" only kills Overlook if Overlook has to replace Slack. It doesn't have to.

01

An overlay, not a migration.

Overlook reads and writes the chat you already use. Channels stay where they are; rooms, rings, orders and the ledger live in a layer above them. Nobody else on the team has to switch for it to work.

02

Useful to one person first.

The buyer is one person running ten agents with no time. They get value on day one, alone: the needs-you column and the brief work even if they're the only one using Overlook. Teams come later, by invitation.

03

An open ring format.

A ring is a tiny typed request any agent can emit: who's asking, which role decides, what's at stake, whether it can be undone, what it relied on. Publish the format openly — every agent framework that speaks it brings Overlook new users.

{
  "ring": "approval",
  "from": "agent:sable",
  "decides": "role:admin",
  "where": "#cust-halcyon › data-migration",
  "ask": "Allow a 24-hour TTL on invoices staging",
  "reversible": true,
  "evidence": ["kb:pii-retention", "msg:8f21c"],
  "order_eligible": true

04

The ledger is the moat.

Every decision, order and knowledge entry, with receipts, adds up to a record of human oversight of AI agents. Auditors and regulators are starting to ask for exactly that — the EU AI Act, for one, sets human-oversight duties for high-risk systems. The record compounds per org, and the basis graph makes it load-bearing: it's what lets one correction fix forty artifacts, a fork preview a decision, and a single command explain any line. It's the one thing an incumbent can't copy by shipping a feature: it's your history.

Priced per supervised agent, not per seat. The cost grows with the agents creating the work, and stays nearly free for the people who only read it.

Dear Slack — and buzz.xyz,

You taught the world that work happens in channels, and we have loved you since the IRC days. But the agent era broke the metaphor. Ten agents, six projects, three customers — and suddenly everything important lives in a thread nobody opens, a DM nobody sees, or a badge that never goes away.

So we sketched the interface we wish existed. Columns instead of tabs, so parallel work stays visible. A room per project instead of a thread per panic. Humans and agents on the same roster, under the same rules — with a gauge on every agent, because they aren't us. Knowledge that files itself, with the receipt attached and a date to check it by.

And attention, finally honest about itself: a dot when something is new, a ring when something needs you, and never a third thing that blinks. Then the part we're proudest of — answer a question once, and keep the receipt, so the same ring never has to come twice.

It's a sketch, not a product — no backend, no pitch deck. (If you want the moat, it's the ledger; we drew you the map.) The ideas are free. Steal them. All of them. We'll cheer from the front row.
