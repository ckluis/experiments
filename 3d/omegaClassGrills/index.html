omegaClass

One cabinet. Every fire.

A wide, sealed stainless cabinet that opens into a full outdoor fire station — a prep + sink counter, a brasero-fed Argentine parrilla and a vertical rack smoker, under two lift-up roofs. Laid out so an asador and a pitmaster can both cook at once.

The idea

Closed, it’s furniture.
Open, it’s a fire station.

Serious outdoor cooks buy one fire, then build a kitchen around it — counters, a sink, shelter — and it never packs away. omegaClass puts the whole station in one stainless cabinet that locks shut, weatherproof, when you’re done.

Three zones · two masters

Each cook gets
their own fire.

An asador and a pitmaster want different things. One wants a brasero, a rake and a crank grate. The other wants sealed, insulated racks and a fire he can tend without opening the chamber. omegaClass lays them out side by side, left to right, with a shared prep counter, so neither has to wait for the other.

A Shared · ~60 cm

Prep, sink & storage

The landing counter an asador needs beside the fire and the wrap station a pitmaster needs beside the smoker, built as one surface.

  • Butcher-block counter, ~92 cm
  • Stainless sink + gooseneck
  • Cutting board
  • Cabinets, drawers, tool hooks

B The asador · ~90 cm

Brasero & crank parrilla

  1. Burn wood down to embers in a separate ~35 cm brasero.
  2. Rake glowing coals sideways under the grate.
  3. Crank the ~55 cm V-channel grate through ~15 cm to set the heat.
  4. Plate on the counter right beside it. Fat drains to a grease cup.

C The pitmaster · ~90 cm

Vertical rack smoker

  1. Load three stacked racks through dual doors hinged at the outer edges.
  2. Seal the gasketed doors on an insulated double-wall chamber.
  3. Tend the firebox below without opening the chamber. Smoke rises through the food.
  4. Wrap on the shared counter. Low racks run hotter, high racks cooler.

Overhead

Two roofs. Raise only the one you’re using.

The top of the cabinet is two independent lids. Each one hinges at the top-back and lifts up and forward about 100° on visible gas struts. Raised, it becomes a roof over the cook, front edge at ~230 cm, with a task light underneath. Lowered, it seals the top against a weather gasket.

The specification

Built to outlast
the people cooking on it.

Marine stainless, folded, TIG-welded and polished into one carcass. The numbers below come from the concept plan; performance figures are design targets until a prototype measures them.

Fuel

A charcoal and wood purist’s station. Wood store under the brasero.

Engineering, in three ideas

01

Heavy low, light high

The carcass, smoker chamber, fireboxes and stored fuel sit low in a 240 × 68 cm footprint. The raised lids are light panels, so they add height without moving the center of gravity much.

02

A smoker that drafts itself

Fire below, racks above, flue on top: the stack effect pulls air in low and pushes smoke out high. Intake and flue dampers meter the burn.

ΔP ≈ g · H · (ρambient − ρflue)

03

Insulation that saves fuel

A ceramic-fiber double wall (k ≈ 0.06 W/m·K) keeps heat in the chamber. Target: ~50–75% less fuel than an uninsulated thin-wall smoker at the same temperature — to be verified by calorimetry.

Why it doesn’t exist yet

Everyone sells a piece.
Nobody sells the station.

Each reference product does one thing very well. omegaClass keeps the best idea from each and puts them in one cabinet that closes. The originality is in the integration, not in any single hinge or grate.
